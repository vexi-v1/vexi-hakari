A live options venue, every fix checked against the bound

Why a venue. The one input HAKARI's bound cannot read for itself is the exposure settling on a price: every position, every protocol. On a fully collateralised options venue it is readable, because every contract on an expiry is an ERC-6909 token with a public supply. So this is the one place the bound can be checked end to end against real price fixes, from the collector here and from ExposureGuard on-chain. The venue is our own, on testnet 46630 (built before the hackathon; none of its code is in this repo): it sells physically settled 15-minute options and fixes each settlement price once, from a 300-second TWAP of its own hookless Uniswap v4 pool on the official PoolManager.

What this is not: a market. The tokens are mocks, every pool holds one full-range position we seeded, all open interest was written by one address (the venue's own) and all swaps came from its own swap adapter. What it shows is the pipeline on a real consumer's contracts, and one result about rate limiters.

Refused: what it means, and what happens next

Some background first. At expiry the venue reads one price, the fix, and pays every contract on that expiry by it. The money riding on that one number is the exposure: here, contracts × the fix price, every series on the expiry summed. The bound is how much money a pool's price can carry before faking it pays for itself: HAKARI prices the cheapest push of the pool held through the fix window, for moves of 0.5 to 20 % both ways, and finds the largest exposure at which no push earns more than it costs. Below the bound a fake loses money; at the bound it breaks even; above it, it earns. Refused means the exposure is at or above that line.

What does "refused" actually do?

Nothing is paid by this price at this moment. SafeSettle answers trusted = false with no price to settle on (tickUsed is 0) and writes the reason on-chain in its Settled event: the exposure, the bound, and the push that set the bound, its size and direction. No position changes, nothing is taken from anyone, and the pool itself is untouched. It is a verdict about the price, not about any trader. What the caller does with the verdict is the next question.

Why refuse, rather than use the price and move on?

Because at or above the bound a fake is profitable: push the pool, let the fix read the pushed price, collect more from the settlement than the push cost. Paying by that price would hand the difference to whoever pushed, taken from everyone on the other side of the contracts. On the HIMS weekend the pool read 54.50 for a stock that had closed at 28.84, and with minting shut nobody could trade it back. A refusal there costs the holders a wait; a settlement costs them the difference.

What happens after a refusal, and who decides?

HAKARI does not decide; the protocol that asked does, and it has three usual moves. Wait and ask again. The bound follows the pool, so when liquidity returns or arbitrage reopens the same settlement passes: in the HIMS replay the bound was 115 USDG at 23:53 and back above 5,000 within about 75 minutes of Monday's first mint. Settle on something slower or from elsewhere: a longer window, a second pool, a reference from outside the chain. Never get there: cap what may be written on a price before expiry so the exposure stays under the bound, which is the depth cap in this venue's own spec. One warning: whichever fallback is chosen is where the next attack goes (see forcing a refusal, below).

On this venue, what happens when the board says refused?

Today, nothing. The venue's fix does not read the bound and never reverts; every fix so far went through, and all 39 with anything at stake were below the bound anyway. This board is a replay of what the bound would have said, and ExposureGuard on-chain makes the same read and emits Checked, but it cannot stop a fix. Wiring the bound into the venue, as a cap on how much it writes per expiry and a badge on a thin fix, is in the venue's own spec, after the hackathon.

Why does the bound itself refuse, and what is "watch"?

The bound is the exposure at which the cheapest fake earns exactly what it costs. One unit more and the fake pays, so the rule refuses from the line, not above it: SafeSettle settles only strictly below. "Watch", from half the bound, is this board's own label and not a rule in any contract: it flags a fix that would be refused if the pool thinned by half, which the HIMS pool did in under an hour on 2026-08-30.

Who is worse off when a settlement is refused?

Whoever was waiting to be paid waits longer, and nobody's position moves. The cost shows up if the fallback is a slower price: in a genuine crash or squeeze a slow price lags, and holders on the right side of a real move are paid on a stale number. That is the trade-off HAKARI is built around, and why it refuses only when the pool is measurably cheap to push, not on every weekend. On the weekend of 2026-09-18 the twelve stock pools with a Friday baseline kept their depth while minting was shut, so a calendar rule would have refused what the book could carry.

Can someone force a refusal?

Yes. A rule that refuses when a pool is thin hands a lever to anyone who can make it look thin at settlement: a liquidity provider pulling out just before expiry, or a push into a thin stretch of the book, since the bound is read from the price now. The refusal itself does no damage, but the fallback the protocol then takes is the next target, which is why choosing it is the protocol's decision and not HAKARI's.

What does a refusal look like on-chain?

One transaction on testnet 46630: a thin pool pushed +35 % and held there, 1,000,000 tokens asked to settle on its 60-second TWAP, and SafeSettle answering that the pool could safely carry 0.0000032 tokens. Its log reads Settled(raw 3000, truncated 1250, trusted false, …) with the 1,823-tick move up that set the bound: the transaction ↗. The README's cast line decodes it.

What you are looking at

  • S* is the venue's own fix: a rate-limited ring of a handful of TWAP observations over the last 300 s before expiry. The limiter clips a big atomic push and passes a small push held through the window untouched; on a single full-range position the cost per move is nearly the same at every rung, so the limiter does not raise the safe line above fee × reserve. A single full-range position is the one book with a closed-form bound, fee × reserve, so it is the calibration case for the lens; a real book has none (HIMS/USDG fell to 0.006× of Friday's bound on 2026-08-30, and on TSLA/USDG a +100 % push undercuts the ladder).
  • On-chain, the same read is ExposureGuard (source): a hook-free consumer of CostModel over the deployed lens, for any pool key. Handed the series ids on an expiry, it sums their supply from the venue, prices the sum at the pool's slot0 and emits Checked(…) with the verdict; it verifies the ids it is given and cannot enumerate them. Against this venue on a 46630 fork (fork test, transcript) its bound equals the table above and the series on the next expiry sum to 0 contracts. It is not deployed on 46630, and nothing in the venue reads it.
  • arbReversionSeconds = 0 because no third-party swap has been seen on these pools: the bound assumes nobody pushes back, which is the conservative setting, not a measurement.
  • HAKARI discloses; it cannot gate the venue's fix. Nothing in the venue reads the bound, and a fix never reverts. The history above is a replay of what the bound would have said (what a refusal does).
